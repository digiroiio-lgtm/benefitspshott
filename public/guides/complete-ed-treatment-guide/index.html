1. What is Erectile Dysfunction?

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is the persistent inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual activity. It affects an estimated 30 million men in the US and over 4.3 million men in the UK. It becomes more prevalent with age — approximately 40% of men at age 40 and up to 70% of men at age 70 are affected to some degree — but ED is not an inevitable consequence of ageing.

ED is also a significant cardiovascular warning sign. Studies show men with ED have a 25% higher risk of major cardiovascular events in the following 10 years, making appropriate diagnosis and treatment important beyond sexual health.

2. Causes of Erectile Dysfunction

ED is broadly categorised by cause:

Vascular (most common — 70–80% of cases)

Impaired blood flow to the penis due to arteriosclerosis, hypertension, high cholesterol, diabetes or metabolic syndrome. The same vascular damage that causes heart disease causes ED — the penile arteries (1–2mm diameter) are far narrower than coronary arteries and show atherosclerotic changes 3–5 years earlier.

Neurological

Nerve damage from prostatectomy, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, spinal cord injury or diabetic neuropathy. Accounts for approximately 10–15% of ED cases.

Hormonal

Low testosterone (hypogonadism), hyperprolactinaemia, or thyroid dysfunction. Often coexists with vascular ED.

Psychogenic

Anxiety, depression, relationship stress, performance anxiety. More common in younger men; often coexists with organic ED in older men.

Drug-induced

Antidepressants (SSRIs), antihypertensives (beta-blockers, thiazides), anti-androgens and recreational drugs can cause or worsen ED.

3. Diagnosis

Accurate diagnosis guides effective treatment. A standard ED assessment includes:

  • Sexual history and IIEF questionnaire — standardised scoring of erectile function
  • Medical and medication history — identifying contributing conditions and drug interactions
  • Blood tests: testosterone, LH, FSH, prolactin, blood glucose (HbA1c), lipid profile
  • Penile Doppler ultrasound — assesses arterial blood flow (PSV and EDV)
  • Blood pressure and cardiovascular risk assessment

4. Treatment Options

Oral PDE5 Inhibitors (Viagra, Cialis, Levitra, Stendra)

First-line treatment for most men with mild to moderate ED. Effective in approximately 70% of unselected patients. Does not work for severe vascular ED or post-radical prostatectomy. Must be taken in advance; not suitable for men on nitrate medications.

Penile Injections (Alprostadil, Trimix)

Highly effective (90%+ response rate) including in men who fail oral medications. Self-injected before intercourse. Psychological barrier to use; priapism risk if dose not managed. Used when oral medications fail.

Vacuum Erection Devices

Non-invasive, low-cost. Produces erection by creating negative pressure. Less natural erection quality; reduces spontaneity. Useful for post-prostatectomy penile rehabilitation.

Testosterone Replacement Therapy

Appropriate for men with confirmed low testosterone. Improves libido and energy significantly; moderate improvement in erectile function. Does not address vascular ED independently.

Penile Implants

Surgical placement of inflatable or malleable prosthesis. Highest patient and partner satisfaction rate (>90%) of all ED treatments. Irreversible; recommended only after other options exhausted. Destroys remaining erectile tissue.

5. Regenerative Treatments: P-Shot & Shockwave Therapy

The most significant advance in ED treatment in the past decade has been the development of regenerative protocols that address the vascular root cause.

P-Shot (Platelet-Rich Plasma)

Blood is drawn, centrifuged to concentrate platelets, and the PRP is injected into penile tissue. Growth factors (PDGF, VEGF, TGF-β) stimulate angiogenesis (new blood vessel formation), smooth muscle regeneration and nerve repair. Results last 12–18 months. Learn more about the P-Shot →

Low-Intensity Shockwave Therapy (Li-ESWT)

Acoustic energy waves applied to the penis trigger neovascularisation and tissue repair. 60–75% IIEF improvement in RCTs. 6–12 sessions over 3–6 weeks. Learn more about shockwave therapy →

Combined Protocol

The combination of P-Shot and shockwave therapy in the same treatment programme produces superior outcomes to either treatment alone. Clinical data shows IIEF improvements of 8–12 points sustained for 18–24 months in good responders.

6. Lifestyle Modification

Evidence strongly supports lifestyle modification as both treatment and prevention for ED:

  • Exercise: 30 min aerobic exercise 3–5× per week improves IIEF by 3–4 points on average
  • Diet: Mediterranean diet reduces ED risk by 40%; reduces cardiovascular risk factors driving vascular ED
  • Weight loss: 10% body weight reduction restores erectile function in ~30% of obese men
  • Smoking cessation: Most effective long-term intervention for vascular ED in smokers
  • Alcohol moderation: Reducing to <14 units/week improves ED in heavy drinkers
  • Sleep optimisation: Testosterone is synthesised during deep sleep; sleep apnoea treatment often resolves ED

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Is ED normal at 40?

Some degree of erectile change is common at 40, but persistent ED is not 'normal' and warrants investigation. It is often the first sign of cardiovascular disease and should be assessed by a doctor.

How quickly do P-Shot results appear?

Most patients begin noticing improvements 4–8 weeks after treatment, with full results at 3–6 months. Some men notice sensitivity changes within the first 2 weeks.

Can I fly to Turkey for treatment?

Yes. Most patients fly in, receive treatment over 2–3 days, and fly home. All-inclusive packages including airport transfers, accommodation and treatment are available. See our Turkey treatment guide for details.

Is P-Shot or shockwave therapy painful?

The P-Shot is performed under local anaesthetic cream; most patients report minimal discomfort. Shockwave therapy involves mild tingling sensations but is not painful. Neither treatment requires sedation.
