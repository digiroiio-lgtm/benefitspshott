Erectile Dysfunction: Causes, Treatments & Costs 2025

A comprehensive, medically reviewed guide to understanding and treating erectile dysfunction — from lifestyle changes to advanced regenerative therapies

◈ Medically Reviewed by Dr. Mehmet Yilmaz, Urologist | January 2025

What is Erectile Dysfunction?

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is the persistent inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual intercourse. While occasional difficulty with erections is normal, ED is diagnosed when the problem occurs consistently — typically in more than 50% of attempts — over a period of at least three months.

ED is one of the most common male sexual health concerns, yet it remains significantly under-reported and under-treated due to embarrassment and stigma. The condition affects not only physical wellbeing but also psychological health, self-esteem, and intimate relationships.

Types and Causes of Erectile Dysfunction

Understanding the underlying cause of ED is essential for selecting the most effective treatment. ED is classified into several categories based on its primary cause:

Vascular (Blood Flow) Causes

Vascular causes account for approximately 50-60% of all ED cases, making them the most common type. The penis requires adequate blood flow to achieve and maintain an erection. Any condition that impairs blood vessel function can therefore contribute to ED:

  • Atherosclerosis: Hardening and narrowing of the arteries reduces blood flow throughout the body, including the penile arteries. ED is often an early sign of systemic cardiovascular disease.
  • Hypertension: High blood pressure damages the arterial walls over time, reducing their ability to dilate and allow increased blood flow during arousal.
  • High cholesterol: Elevated LDL cholesterol promotes plaque formation in arteries, restricting penile blood flow.
  • Venous leakage: In some men, blood flows into the penis normally but leaks out too quickly through the veins, making it difficult to maintain an erection.

Hormonal Causes

Hormonal imbalances, particularly low testosterone (hypogonadism), account for approximately 5-10% of ED cases. Testosterone plays a critical role in libido, energy levels and the physiological mechanisms of erection. Other hormonal causes include:

  • Low testosterone (hypogonadism): Testosterone levels naturally decline with age, falling by approximately 1% per year after age 30. Significantly low levels can reduce sexual desire and impair erection quality.
  • Elevated prolactin: Hyperprolactinaemia, often caused by a pituitary adenoma, suppresses testosterone production and is a rare but treatable cause of ED.
  • Thyroid dysfunction: Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism can disrupt the hormonal environment necessary for normal sexual function.

Neurological Causes

The nervous system plays an essential role in the erectile response. Nerve damage or dysfunction from various conditions can disrupt the signals between brain, spinal cord and penis:

  • Diabetes mellitus: Diabetic neuropathy is one of the most significant causes of neurogenic ED, affecting approximately 50% of men with diabetes. Chronic high blood glucose damages the small nerve fibres responsible for erectile signalling.
  • Multiple sclerosis: Demyelination of the spinal cord and brain pathways involved in erection commonly causes ED in men with MS.
  • Parkinson's disease: Disruption of dopaminergic pathways affects sexual arousal and erection.
  • Spinal cord injury: Depending on the level and completeness of the injury, spinal cord damage frequently results in ED.
  • Post-surgical nerve damage: Radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer and other pelvic surgeries can damage the cavernous nerves, causing post-operative ED in 25-75% of cases.

Psychological Causes

Psychological factors may be the primary cause of ED (psychogenic ED) or may compound physical causes. Pure psychogenic ED is more common in younger men:

  • Performance anxiety: Anxiety about sexual performance creates a self-reinforcing cycle where the fear of ED actually causes or worsens it.
  • Depression: Both depression itself and antidepressant medications (particularly SSRIs) are associated with ED. Depression affects sexual desire, arousal and the neurochemical environment necessary for erection.
  • Relationship issues: Unresolved conflict, poor communication and lack of intimacy can manifest as ED.
  • Stress: Chronic work-related or financial stress elevates cortisol and adrenaline, which constrict blood vessels and suppress sexual arousal.
  • Past sexual trauma: Traumatic sexual experiences can create psychological barriers to sexual function.

Risk Factors for Erectile Dysfunction

Certain lifestyle factors and health conditions significantly increase the risk of developing ED:

  • Age: Prevalence increases substantially with age, though ED is not an inevitable consequence of ageing and is treatable at any age.
  • Diabetes: Men with diabetes are 2-3 times more likely to develop ED, and tend to develop it at a younger age.
  • Cardiovascular disease: ED and heart disease share the same vascular risk factors and often coexist.
  • Obesity: Excess body weight, particularly abdominal obesity, is associated with lower testosterone, insulin resistance and vascular disease — all contributing to ED.
  • Smoking: Tobacco use causes vascular damage and dramatically increases ED risk. Men who smoke are twice as likely to develop ED as non-smokers.
  • Excessive alcohol consumption: Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant that impairs erections in the short term. Chronic heavy drinking causes nerve damage and hormonal disruption.
  • Physical inactivity: Sedentary lifestyle contributes to obesity, cardiovascular disease and depression, all of which increase ED risk.
  • Certain medications: Antidepressants (SSRIs), antihypertensives (beta-blockers, thiazide diuretics), antiandrogens and finasteride can all impair erectile function as side effects.

Diagnosing Erectile Dysfunction

A thorough assessment is essential to identify the underlying cause and guide treatment selection. Diagnosis typically involves:

  • Medical history: Duration of symptoms, frequency of ED, presence of morning erections, medications, smoking/alcohol history, psychological factors.
  • IIEF questionnaire: The International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) is a validated 15-question questionnaire used to quantify the severity of ED. Scores range from 5-25, with scores below 21 indicating varying degrees of ED.
  • Physical examination: Assessment of secondary sexual characteristics, genital examination, blood pressure measurement.
  • Blood tests: Testosterone levels, blood glucose (HbA1c), lipid panel, full blood count, thyroid function.
  • Penile Doppler ultrasound: Assessment of blood flow in the penile arteries, useful for confirming vascular ED.
  • Nocturnal penile tumescence test: Monitoring of erections during sleep to distinguish psychogenic from organic ED.

Conventional ED Treatments

PDE5 Inhibitors (Oral Medications)

Phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitors are the most commonly prescribed first-line treatment for ED. They work by increasing blood flow to the penis in response to sexual stimulation. They do not cause spontaneous erections and require sexual arousal to be effective.

  • Sildenafil (Viagra): Takes effect in 30-60 minutes, lasts 4-6 hours. The original PDE5 inhibitor, highly effective for most types of ED.
  • Tadalafil (Cialis): Onset in 30-60 minutes, lasts up to 36 hours. Available as daily low-dose or on-demand higher doses.
  • Vardenafil (Levitra): Similar to sildenafil but may be effective at lower doses.
  • Avanafil (Spedra): Faster onset than sildenafil (15-30 minutes), fewer drug interactions.

PDE5 inhibitors are effective in approximately 70% of men with ED but are contraindicated with nitrate medications and should not be used without medical supervision in men with cardiovascular disease.

Vacuum Erection Devices

A vacuum erection device (VED) uses negative pressure to draw blood into the penis, with a constriction ring applied at the base to maintain the erection. VEDs are effective and carry minimal side effects, though some men find them cumbersome to use. They are particularly useful for men who cannot tolerate PDE5 inhibitors.

Penile Injections (Intracavernosal Therapy)

Alprostadil injected directly into the corpora cavernosa of the penis produces an erection within 5-15 minutes regardless of sexual stimulation. Effective in over 85% of men but requires self-injection at home. Side effects can include priapism (prolonged erection) and penile pain.

Advanced ED Treatments

P-Shot (Priapus Shot)

The P-Shot is a regenerative treatment that uses platelet-rich plasma (PRP) derived from the patient's own blood. PRP contains concentrated growth factors including VEGF, PDGF and TGF-beta that stimulate angiogenesis, tissue repair and neural regeneration when injected into the penile tissue. Clinical studies demonstrate 70-80% improvement in erectile function, with results lasting 12-18 months. Unlike conventional medications, the P-Shot addresses the underlying tissue damage rather than just masking symptoms.

Low-Intensity Shockwave Therapy (Li-ESWT)

Low-intensity extracorporeal shockwave therapy uses acoustic waves delivered to the penile tissue to stimulate angiogenesis, activate stem cells and release nitric oxide. A typical course consists of 6-12 sessions over 6-12 weeks. Clinical evidence shows 60-75% improvement in IIEF scores, with results sustained for 12-24 months. Shockwave therapy is non-invasive and well-tolerated, making it an excellent option for men preferring to avoid injections or surgery.

When to See a Doctor

You should seek medical advice about erectile dysfunction if:

  • ED occurs persistently (more than 50% of sexual attempts) for more than 3 months
  • ED has developed suddenly, which may indicate a vascular event or medication side effect
  • You have risk factors for cardiovascular disease (diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol, smoking)
  • ED is causing significant distress to you or affecting your relationship
  • You are under 40, as ED in younger men warrants thorough investigation for underlying causes
  • ED is accompanied by other symptoms such as reduced libido, fatigue or breast tissue enlargement, which may indicate hormonal issues

Remember that ED is a medical condition, not a reflection of masculinity or character. Effective treatments are available at any age, and seeking help is the first step to recovery.
